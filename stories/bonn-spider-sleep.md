A jumping spider from northern Australia sleeps in at least two stages, including one that looks a lot like the rapid-eye-movement sleep of vertebrates. ABC Science reported on October 27, 2026, on a study of Portia fimbriata published that day in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

Daniela Rößler, a behavioural ecologist at the University of Bonn and a co-author, said the night split into a still, or quiescent, phase and a later REM-like phase. In the first, the spiders groomed and slowly drooped in their upside-down resting webs. In the second, cameras caught leg curls, eye movements, and twitching. Co-author Nadja Geiger said the species is diurnal, highly visual, and easy to score because its night posture is so clear. When the team played a loud noise, spiders in the still phase took longer to respond than awake spiders, which is one of the standard signs of sleep.

Rößler said the pattern is closer to vertebrate sleep than anyone expected. The paper does not claim the spiders dream. It is a behavioural case, filmed in the lab, and the team has said the next question is how the same spiders sleep in the wild.
