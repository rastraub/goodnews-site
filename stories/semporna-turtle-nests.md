The Star reported on November 14, 2026, that green turtle nesting at Sabah's Turtle Islands has tripled since the 1970s, to more than 20,000 nests a year. Sabah Parks director Maklarin Lakim tied that count to more than five decades of work. The islands have been protected since 1977, and Sabah banned turtle meat and egg consumption in 1997. Globally, the International Union for Conservation of Nature moved green turtles from endangered to least concern last year. Professor Juanita Joseph of Universiti Malaysia Sabah said she cried when she heard it.

The recovery is not even. The same report says the other sea turtle species are still vulnerable or critically endangered, apart from the flatback, whose status is unclear, and that some regional green turtle populations are still endangered. On Omadal, Sitti Rasah Abdul, 50, and a women's cooperative of about 20 people have moved eggs to a hatchery since 2012. They began with WWF and now put 10 percent of their handicraft and catering income into the work. At Mataking Reef Resort, owner Sophia Sie said collected eggs rose from about 600 in 2006 to about 36,000. About one hatchling in 1,000 survives to maturity, around age 20. WWF-Malaysia's Gavin Jolis said the results of today's work will take decades, and that a DNA library called Shellbank is being used to trace where turtles come from. The nesting number is the measured gain. Least concern is not a claim that every beach is safe.
