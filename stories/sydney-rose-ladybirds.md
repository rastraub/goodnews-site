Centennial Park in Sydney is using ladybirds and parasitic wasps, instead of routine chemical sprays, against aphids in its rose garden. ABC News reported the work on November 12, 2026. The rosarium was planted about 1909 by the park director of that time, Joseph Maiden. It now has 33 varieties and about 800 rose plants overlooking Busbys Pond. Senior horticulturalist Tristan Harman said a heavy aphid infestation follows winter pruning. Left alone, he said, the aphids would stop the plants looking like roses. Aphids distort leaves, can pass on viruses, and often leave a path for black mould.

Thousands of parasitic wasps and their eggs were released. The adult lays eggs inside an aphid, the larva eats the aphid from the inside, and an adult wasp later emerges. About 1,000 ladybirds were tipped onto the roses to eat aphids directly. Five batches go out from September to early November. Harman said the insects had already won after the second release, in late September. This is the second year he has run the releases. A South Australian company supplied three kinds of wasps, eggs, and live ladybirds. This year's insects cost about 1,300 Australian dollars. Harman said spraying for aphids also kills bees and other insects the garden is not trying to kill. He put yearly visits to the park at about 17 million people, including wedding parties, and said staff try to keep the garden at a high standard without spraying unless they have to. The result he described is this season's roses, after those releases. It is not a promise for every pest in the park.
