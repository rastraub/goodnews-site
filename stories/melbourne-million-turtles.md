A citizen project that set out to give a million native freshwater turtles a better chance has a running tally, and the rescues are the part a neighbor can see. ABC News reported on November 21, 2026, that 1 Million Turtles, launched in 2021, has taken close to 40,000 records from people who log turtles in the wild. James Van Dyke, an associate professor at La Trobe University and a co-founder, said the latest count of turtles saved from roads and other hazards is 3,082. Volunteers have also found and protected 2,468 nests.

Van Dyke was plain that the million is arithmetic, not a head count. If each saved turtle lived another 30 years and half of them laid about 20 eggs a year, he said, that could approach 900,000 eggs. Protected nests add more: an average nest of about 20 eggs would be roughly 50,000 eggs already watched. Deb Bower, at the University of New England, put the records at about 38,000, counting live turtles, dead turtles, and nests. Most of the reports come from Victoria, New South Wales, and South Australia, with another cluster around Perth and Albany.

The project follows three inland species in particular: the eastern long-necked turtle, the broad-shelled turtle, and the Murray River short-necked turtle. Radio tags on 50 hatchlings showed how often that stage ends in a bird's meal. Van Dyke said most of those tracked hatchlings were eaten, because the team finds chewed transmitters under trees. He also said those hatchlings are food for native birds. A wet spring in the southeast is useful for the adults, Bower said, because females need food and places to nest. November is when they leave the water to lay, sometimes only a few metres from the bank and sometimes 100 metres or more, which is when cars meet them.
