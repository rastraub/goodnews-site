Mongabay reported on November 5, 2026, that new mapping from Embrapa, Brazil's agricultural research agency, shows cacao spreading in the Amazon mostly onto land that was cleared years ago. In Pará, the country's top producing state, the mapped cacao area grew by about a third between 2022 and 2024, to 116,436 hectares. About 96 percent of the cacao mapped in Pará, and 98 percent in Rondônia, sits on land deforested before 2020. Adriano Venturieri, a remote-sensing specialist at Embrapa Amazônia Oriental in Belém, led the work, which grows out of TerraClass, a program with the space agency INPE that maps how cleared Amazon land is used.

The pattern of planting has changed. Until the 2022 map, the cacao Embrapa had identified in Pará was in agroforestry, under other trees. Almost two-thirds of the newer plantings are full-sun monocultures. Those fields can yield more beans per tree, and they are more exposed to pests and disease, with a farm depending on a single crop. Mongabay noted that the older-clearing pattern lines up with European Union rules on deforestation. The helpful finding is where the crop is going: old pasture and other land opened before 2020, not a fresh cut of rainforest for most of the mapped area. The monoculture shift is a separate limit. The map is not a promise that every new cacao field is shaded, or that the Amazon is no longer being cleared for other reasons.
