Researchers in Australia published illustrated keys to the lanternfishes in the country's waters, Phys.org reported on November 20, 2026. The paper, in Technical Reports of the Australian Museum, covers 30 genera and 141 species inside Australia's exclusive economic zone. CSIRO's account says no region-wide guide had existed for naming them to species. Worldwide there are at least 254 lanternfish species, in every ocean from the Arctic to the Antarctic.

They are small. The largest reach about 280 millimetres and the smallest about 25. Light organs called photophores dot the body and head, and the arrangement of those spots is the most useful way to tell species apart. Most lanternfishes live at 200 to 1,000 metres, in the dim mesopelagic zone. The lights are thought to signal, to find food or a mate, and to break up a silhouette against faint light from above. Many species also swim up toward the surface at night and back down by day. The article calls that the largest daily migration of any animal.

The same piece cites an estimate of about 5 gigatonnes for lanternfish biomass worldwide, roughly 50 times the annual commercial catch of all marine species. That figure is presented as an existing estimate, not a new weighing from this guide. Tuna, penguins, albatrosses, seals, whales, and dolphins eat them. The news is a key that lets other people name the fish they already catch in nets and surveys. It is not a claim that the populations have grown.
