Divers off Dahme are moving seagrass shoots by hand, and a meadow planted in the Baltic in 2021 is still thick. DW reported on October 28, 2026, that about half a dozen divers were working in cold water off the beach town, lifting shoots with the roots intact from a donor meadow and bagging them for a new site. They take only a few from each spot. The young plants need sand, shelter from waves, and enough light.

Myriam Spicka, who leads the NGO Seagrass Conservation, said she has watched the meadows change across more than 30 years of diving. Scientists estimate that about 60 percent of the Baltic coast's seagrass is gone. Thorsten Reusch of the GEOMAR Helmholtz Centre in Kiel called that the bad news, and said the good news is that the decline has stopped. The meadows feed fish and crabs, slow waves, and store carbon in the seabed much faster than forests, DW reported, on the order of 30 to 50 times. That storage rate is a scientific estimate, not a claim about one new patch off Dahme.

Planting shoot by shoot is slow. Reinhard Helbig's start-up ZosteraTec collects fresh seagrass washed ashore after storms, grows it, and binds strands with biodegradable thread on the beach so less of the work happens underwater. GEOMAR is also testing seed. Esther Thomsen said about 10 percent of plants in their meadows make seeds, and that seed adds genetic variety. She and colleagues checked the first Baltic meadow Germany planted in 2021, counting shoots against the donor bed. She said it looked dense. A dense check of one meadow is not a restored coastline.
