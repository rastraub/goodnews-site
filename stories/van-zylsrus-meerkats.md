Adult meerkats moved a short distance inside their own land found the way back without the group, Phys.org reported on November 19, 2026. Mathilde Martin, Benjamin Robira, Matéa Rossi, and Marta Manser of the University of Zurich, with volunteers at the Kalahari Research Centre, published the work in the Journal of Experimental Biology. They fitted a few animals at a time with small collars, then carried one foraging meerkat 1.5 kilometres away and set it down still inside the territory.

Of 20 adults from five groups, 18 left the release point. All 18 of those homed, reaching a sleeping burrow, the capture point, or the group directly. Seventy percent rejoined the group on their own. Two males stayed near the release site and were returned after 90 minutes. Some animals started within minutes. Others circled for a while, then held a fairly straight line, pausing to look or to sniff. Groups keep more than one burrow, so arriving at a hole was not always the same as finding the family, and the search continued.

The paper says differences in how well they did were not explained by sex or by experience. Daily life in the group seems to teach the route. The study is about that skill in the adults who were tested. It does not say every pup can do the same thing, and it does not change how the groups are protected.
