Beekeepers in Tibet had noticed varroa mites seeming to vanish from hives, and a study tried to find out why. Phys.org reported on November 20, 2026, on a paper published November 19 in PLOS Biology by Huoqing Zheng at Zhejiang University and colleagues. The team split 60 infested honey bee colonies into four groups of 15 and moved them to four heights in Tibet and in Yunnan, in southern China. Colonies kept above 3,500 metres, about 11,500 feet, in Lhasa had the best two-year record: 13 of 15 were still alive. The colonies at the other sites did not last longer than a year.

Mites at the higher sites reproduced less, and their offspring developed more slowly. The same drop in reproduction showed up when the researchers created low-oxygen conditions in hives that were not at altitude. The authors' hypothesis is that the thin air of the plateau is what limits the mite. They said the result needs to be repeated in other settings. The reason two Lhasa colonies died was not recorded, because those hives were already empty at the check. The paper is a physiological limit on the mite, observed in this trial. It is not a method a beekeeper can order, and it does not say every high-altitude hive is safe.
