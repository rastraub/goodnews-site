Mongabay reported on November 18, 2026, that a study in Global Change Biology mapped how jaguars still move across the Americas. Researchers used GPS collars on 172 cats in Argentina, Belize, Brazil, Colombia, Costa Rica, Mexico, Paraguay, and Peru. They marked 307 core areas, covering more than 7.75 million square kilometres, and 176 corridors between them, plus 507 more possible links among 905 protected areas and Indigenous territories. The largest core, about 6.5 million square kilometres, runs through the Amazon, the Llanos, the Chaco, and the Pantanal. Lead author Guilherme Costa Alvarenga, of the University of Oxford and the Mamirauá Institute in Tefé, said the last range-wide connectivity study was 15 years old and did not use collar data. The paper says jaguars have lost about half their historical range.

The local case in the same report is Tape'ỹ, a male found on a porch in Foz do Iguaçu in June 2026. He was sedated and later released in Iguaçu National Park. Yara de Melo Barros, of the Onças do Iguaçu Project, said that park's Green Corridor holds about a third of the fewer than 300 jaguars left in the Atlantic Forest, and that a recovering group is still at risk if it stays cut off. A separate effort, the Jaguar Rivers Initiative, brings together groups in Argentina, Bolivia, Paraguay, and Brazil to protect and reconnect about 1,200 square kilometres of the Paraná basin. Its first three years are budgeted at $80 million. A first boat survey left Corumbá in September. Alvarenga said the map is a first step, and that field checks of whether jaguars actually use the corridors are still ahead. The corridors on the map are places to check, not trails already secured.
