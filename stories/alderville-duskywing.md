The mottled duskywing is a small butterfly most people have never seen, and a team in Ontario is trying to make its return last. CBC's What on Earth newsletter reported on November 19, 2026, that habitat loss had nearly wiped the species out in the province. Scientists are bringing it back to places such as Pinery Provincial Park and the Alderville Black Oak Savanna, which holds Canada's largest population. Ryan Norris, a University of Guelph biologist leading the research, said many butterfly moves around the world have been a release followed by hope, with no monitoring. This project is trying to learn what actually keeps a population going.

Rick Beaver, a knowledge keeper for Alderville First Nation and a former director of research at the savanna, credits the community's controlled burns. The caterpillars depend on New Jersey tea, a shrub with fluffy white flowers, and that shrub is itself tied to fire. Burns keep other plants from smothering it. Parts of the savanna are burned every year. One section has not burned in about a decade and will be used to compare. Gillian di Petta, acting natural heritage manager, called the pairing of local knowledge and measurement two-eyed seeing. Norris said the duskywing was chosen because small populations were already in Ontario and the host plant was easy to find. It has been reintroduced in several places, including a former tobacco field in Norfolk County. The next species on the list are the frosted elfin, absent from Ontario since the early 1990s and being brought from New York to the north shore of Lake Erie, and the curiously isolated hairstreak, known in Canada from Waterton Lakes National Park in Alberta. Those next steps are underway as reintroductions, not finished recoveries.
