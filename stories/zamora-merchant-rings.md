A handful of small metal rings, easy to miss beside the bells and needles of Mesoamerican sites, may have been how a long-distance trader showed who he was. Phys.org reported on November 26, 2026, on a study in the journal Ancient Mesoamerica by Laura Solar-Valverde, an archaeologist at El Colegio de Michoacán in Zamora.

Metalworking spread into western Mexico from Central and South America around the year 900. Among the bells, needles, earrings, and fishhooks, some sites also held a ring: a face with a long nose and a two-lobed feathered headdress. Solar-Valverde reads that face as Yacatecuhtli or Ek Chuah, gods of trade in Aztec and Maya tradition. The rings turn up from the hills of Hidalgo to the coast of Belize, the Pacific shore of Guatemala, and the borderlands of El Salvador. They are small, about 1.8 to 2.5 centimetres across, sized for an adult finger. One from Cerro del Teúl was still on a buried person's hand. Sites that have them usually have only one or two, while bells and needles show up by the dozen. The same places also held goods from far away, including Plumbate pottery, stone vessels, turquoise, shell, and other metal.

Sixteenth-century accounts say traders carried several marks, including rings, and then stop. They do not describe the rings. Solar-Valverde's point is that people who have been inferred from the goods they moved can be pictured as individuals. The link to merchants is an argument from the look of the rings, where they were found, and those brief texts. It is not a label that was still attached to every ring.
