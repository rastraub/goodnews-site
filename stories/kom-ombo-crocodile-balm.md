Ancient embalmers at one town on the Nile seem to have kept a recipe for crocodile mummies across centuries. Phys.org reported on November 27, 2026, on a paper in Scientific Reports by Tabea J. Koch and colleagues, working with mummies now in London, including at the British Museum.

Egyptians mummified thousands of crocodiles linked with Sobek, a god tied to the Nile, fertility, and royal power. One burial from Kom Ombo, dated to the middle of the first millennium BC, is hard to miss: more than 25 hatchlings were mounted on reeds along the back and neck of a crocodile nearly 4 metres long. The arrangement echoes the way living young ride on an adult. Beeswax and pine resin or tar are still in the balm on that adult and on two of the hatchlings. Centuries later, embalmers at the same site used the same combination on another adult crocodile.

Two juvenile crocodiles from Manfalut, farther north, carried a balm made mainly of cedar tar. The researchers asked whether size or workplace explained the difference, and they argue for a local tradition centered at Kom Ombo. They tested seven mummies and 22 samples, including stained wrapping and a dark crust on the skin. Three groups of very young crocodiles had no recorded burial place. The shared recipe is evidence from these mummies. It is not a claim that every workshop along the Nile worked the same way.
