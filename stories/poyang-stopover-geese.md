Waterbirds that pause at more places on the way north do a better job of meeting an earlier spring, Phys.org reported on November 19, 2026. Leiden ecologist Yali Si and colleagues tracked 220 spring migrations by five species of waterbirds that winter near Poyang Lake in China and breed toward Siberia. The Journal of Animal Ecology paper covers nine years of GPS tags that logged a position every one to two hours. Greater white-fronted geese and tundra bean geese are among the birds shown wearing the tags.

In spring, fresh growth starts in the south and moves north. Si described the birds as surfing that green wave, feeding on young plants and using stopovers to wait or to push on. The tracks showed that birds with more stopovers adjusted their arrival to the start of growth on the breeding grounds more closely. Chicks need that timing. Summers in the Arctic are short, and a late hatch can miss the peak of food.

Si said many stopovers sit in crowded landscapes, and wetlands in particular are turned into farmland or broken into smaller pieces. Losing one link changes the whole route. The study is evidence for protecting that chain. It does not announce a new reserve, and it does not measure how many of these birds succeeded in raising young.
