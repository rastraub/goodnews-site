Museum curators believe a heavy curved bone picked up on the Blackwater shore near Tollesbury came from a woolly rhinoceros. BBC News reported the opinion on November 17, 2026. Paul Rowe, 63, found it in May while helping with a bird survey along the sea wall. At a geology event earlier this month, Neil Adams, curator of mammal fossils at the Natural History Museum, said the animal most likely lived between 30,000 and 60,000 years ago. The bone looked too small for a mammoth and too large for a bison. Roula Pappa, senior curator of fossil mammals, agreed after a first look that it is part of a pelvis.

Woolly rhinos died out when the climate warmed more than 10,000 years ago. Adults could reach about 3.5 metres long. Rowe said a find like this usually comes from a dig or a gravel pit, and that he would rather see the bone verified and displayed than keep it. Mark Curteis, former curator at Chelmsford Museum, called it interesting and unusual, and not a first: Ice Age mammal remains wash out of gravels along the Essex coast, including at Clacton, Holland-on-Sea, Walton-on-the-Naze, and Mersea. The identification is that expert reading. It is not yet a published lab result.
