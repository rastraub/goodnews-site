Scientists can now compare the height of forests from Borneo to a dry woodland using one shared set of maps. Phys.org reported on November 27, 2026, that a team led by the University of Bristol has published the Global Canopy Atlas in Nature Ecology and Evolution. It joins 3,458 airborne laser surveys from 56 countries. More than 100 institutions took part over five years.

LiDAR, the laser method, is flown on an airplane. It draws the leaves, the branches, and the ground underneath across large stretches of land. Tommaso Jucker, professor of forest ecology at Bristol and a co-lead author, said the canopy regulates life on Earth and that researchers had lacked one harmonized three-dimensional picture of it. The paper's examples run from tall tropical rainforest to sparse dry woodland, including a human-shaped landscape in Malaysian Borneo where oil palm sits next to dipterocarp trees that reach about 80 metres.

The atlas is meant to help map carbon in woody ecosystems and to guide research on biodiversity and restoration. It is a measurement. It does not, by itself, set aside a forest or stop a clearance.
