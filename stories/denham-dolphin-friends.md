Dolphins in Shark Bay that kept the strongest and most stable social groups also lived the longest, The Guardian reported on November 25, 2026. The study, in PLOS Biology, uses nearly 40 years of observations of Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphins. Observers recognized individuals by color, place, and the marks on the back and dorsal fin, alone and in groups. The species can live more than 50 years.

Lead author Kelley Meehan, of the University of Queensland, said group size varies, and that older dolphins become more selective about which groups they join. She said company can help animals find partners, share information, and form bonds, and that it can also spread disease or add stress. In this population, a preference for smaller groups late in life likely reflects the loss of close companions. At every stage, males spent time in larger groups than females. Meehan said male alliances are how males consort with a female, so those bonds matter to reproduction. Females spent more time with kin and with dolphins that forage in similar ways. Their group size was less predictable. Co-author Celine Frere, also at the University of Queensland, said the long record is what made the comparison possible. The finding is about this bay. It is not a claim that friendship lengthens the life of every dolphin.
