The Conversation published on November 13, 2026, an account by geographers and sustainability scientists who interviewed growers, conservation staff, and wine experts in the Western Cape and reviewed practices at 107 vineyards, about 20 percent of a regional total of more than 500. The Cape produces about 90 percent of South Africa's wine and holds the Cape Floral Region, including fynbos found nowhere else. Temperatures there have risen 1.5 to 2 times the global average over 50 years, and a three-year drought in the late 2010s cut grape production.

The authors write that many operators now treat soil and habitat as part of staying in business, not as land taken out of production. Most of the country's vineyards take part in the Integrated Production of Wine system, started with researchers in 1998, which sets rules for pesticides, water, wastewater, and stewardship and uses independent audits. Growers described restoring native plants along waterways, removing alien species, planting cover crops such as legumes, oats, rye, radish, and mustard, and using compost and mulch. Several said they watch pests and spray less broadly, because chemicals cost more. WWF Conservation Champions and other certifications were part of the encouragement. The paper also says vineyard area has decreased, exports and labour are expensive, and some growers are changing crops or adding tourism. Students Zoe Van der Walt, Jack Spining, and Dryver Finch contributed. The finding is how these farms describe their choices. It is not a measured yield increase.
