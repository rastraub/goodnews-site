Two purpose-built houses at the University of York are giving a first look at what a tighter building does to the air inside, and the early answer cuts both ways. The Guardian reported on November 27, 2026, that the Interiors facility has one house built like a typical UK home of the last thirty years and another built as an energy-efficient house of the near future, with triple glazing, extra wall and loft insulation, and ventilation that recycles heat from the air leaving the building. Pipes in the ceilings run to instruments in a control room. People can cook, sit, and sleep in the houses. They are still a laboratory.

Early results found less outdoor particle pollution in the future house than in the typical one. In the hot summer, the future house averaged 5 degrees Celsius warmer, and indoor temperatures regularly stayed above 25 degrees. Lia Chatzidiakou of the team said airtight buildings can cut carbon footprints and can help with cold, damp housing, but they can also trap pollutants, and that without ventilation and shading they can trade a winter problem for summer overheating. Nicola Carslaw, the director, said designs have to treat energy, ventilation, indoor air, and how people actually live as one problem. The news is a measurement from houses that are already standing. It is not a building code, and it does not say the efficient house is healthier in every season.
