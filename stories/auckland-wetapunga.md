New Zealand's wētāpunga, often called the world's heaviest insect, is now classed as nationally increasing. The Guardian reported on October 5, 2026, on a paper in the New Zealand Journal of Zoology by honorary Department of Conservation researcher Chris Green. A breeding program over about 15 years has released around 11,000 insects. A female can weigh as much as a mouse, with a body up to 10 centimeters long and a leg span up to 20 centimeters.

The insects are presumed extinct in the wild on the mainland. They held on mainly on predator-free islands in the Hauraki Gulf and off Northland. Auckland Zoo and Butterfly Creek raised nymphs on foliage, fish-flake protein, and leaf litter, then moved them once they were large enough to cope. Green counted 45 translocations to islands and to some mainland reserves north of Auckland. Monitoring on two islands found self-sustaining populations, with fourth and fifth generations born there. The Guardian corrected an earlier line: the new status is nationally increasing, not nationally recovering. The paper is about this insect's recovery, not a claim that New Zealand's other threatened species are in the same position.
