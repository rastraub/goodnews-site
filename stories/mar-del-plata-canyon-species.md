A canyon off Argentina yielded 57 species new to science in the time it took twenty taxonomists to sit with the jars. Mongabay reported on October 13, 2026, that the Nippon Foundation-Nekton Ocean Census workshop in Buenos Aires also logged five new genera. Most specimens came from a 2025 Schmidt Ocean Institute expedition on the research vessel Falkor (too), when the remotely operated vehicle SuBastian livestreamed dives in the Mar del Plata Canyon. Ocean Census said more than 4 million people watched. Many came for a plump orange sea star that looked like Patrick Star. It was not a new species. The team left it where it was.

The workshop's tally was 22 new echinoderms, including sea cucumbers and feather stars, 20 copepods, 14 corals and sea pens, and one tusk-shelled mollusk. A horned sea cucumber nicknamed Loki was filmed at 2,577 meters. Two of the coral finds may be new genera, and one may be a new family, still waiting on genetics. Ocean Census counts a species as discovered once experts agree it is new and enter it in an open database. A formal description, with a museum specimen and a paper, can take years. Daniel Lauretta of Argentina's CONICET, who led the 2025 expedition, said cooperation matters because few specialists work on some of these groups. The team examined about 10 percent of the copepod samples, and every specimen in that slice was new, leaving about 90 percent still unopened.

The canyon sits where the warm Brazil Current meets the cold Malvinas Current, and it drops more than 3,500 meters. It is not clean. A study of the same dives recorded 29 pieces of litter, mostly plastic and some rubber, including a plastic bag with a sea star on it. The specimens will stay in Argentina, including at the Argentine Museum of Natural Sciences in Buenos Aires. Lauretta's team plans to return with Schmidt Ocean Institute in February and March 2027 to a different canyon. Fifty-seven logged species are a workshop result, not a finished catalog.
