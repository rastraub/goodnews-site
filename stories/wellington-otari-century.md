Ōtari-Wilton's Bush has marked 100 years since it opened as a native-plant garden, RNZ reported on November 24, 2026. The official opening was October 12, 1926. The Wellington park now covers more than 105 hectares, about 100 of them protected podocarp-broadleaf forest and about five a curated collection. Trust member Kathy Ombler said the idea from the start was to bring native plants there to protect them.

The valley's protection is older than the garden. The Wilton family arrived in Port Nicholson in October 1841, when Job Wilton was six. By 1860 he was farming in the Kaiwharawhara Valley and, against the habit of the time, fenced about 17 acres of forest away from stock and axes. Ombler said that left original northern rātā podocarp forest that was never cut. Māori knew the valley as Ōtari-kākā, the place of snares for kākā. It was gazetted as a forest reserve in 1906.

Botanist Leonard Cockayne then pressed the government and the city for an open-air native plant museum, at a time when public gardens were planted with English roses and clipped hedges. Trust chair Carol West said his plan had four parts: bring native plants from around the country for study, recreate ecosystems, restore the forest already there, and show that native plants belong in gardens. The centenary is that history and the forest still standing. It is not a new species list.
