A seaweed collected once in 1935, and then known mostly as scraps of young plants, has turned up as forests. Phys.org reported on November 20, 2026, on a study in the journal Ecology by Matthew Rees, Nathan Knott, and colleagues. Botanist A. H. S. Lucas named Sargassum howeanum from large adults taken near Lord Howe Island. For the next 90 years, surveys in the shallows mostly found small, immature plants. Remote cameras at 20 to 38 metres, about 66 to 125 feet, found dense stands. Algal specialist Gerald Kraft identified the tangled specimens as Sargassum spinuligerum and S. howeanum. The howeanum plants were adults up to 72 centimetres long, with the receptacles they use to reproduce.

Video from marine-park surveys in 2009, 2013, 2017, and 2022 showed the area covered by Sargassum forests had grown about 22 times between 2009 and 2022. The authors do not claim to know why. Sea surface temperatures at the island have risen about 0.2 degrees Celsius a decade since 1985, they wrote, while satellite data suggest water at 20 to 50 metres was cooler ahead of the 2022 survey than in earlier years. They offer that cooler deep water as a possible refuge, and they say the idea is preliminary. Lord Howe lies about 600 kilometres off the eastern coast. The finding is this spread in the survey footage, not a forecast for every seaweed forest.
