BBC News reported on November 17, 2026, that researchers at the University of St Andrews have read humpback whale genomes from breath collected by drone off northern British Columbia. The study, in Molecular Ecology Resources, describes 58 blow samples from 26 whales in Gitga'at First Nation territory. The fieldwork used a research station on Fin Island. A sterile dish on the aircraft passed through the exhaled spray, so the team did not have to dart the animal for this sample. The nuclear genomes are low coverage, about 2.3 times the genome on average. That is enough for questions about population and kinship. It is not a finished medical file for each whale. Mitochondrial genomes from the same breaths were much deeper.

The paper checks the breath results against earlier genetic profiles and against tissue, including two biopsies taken in 2022. BBC News said the aim is to learn whether whales that forage together are relatives or long-term companions. Before this project, only one other North Pacific humpback whole genome was public. The news is a less invasive way to read these particular whales. It is not a new census, and the authors say the method will not work equally well for every species.
