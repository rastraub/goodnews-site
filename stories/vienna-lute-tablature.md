Music written for the Renaissance lute, in a notation almost nobody can read anymore, is opening on a public website. Phys.org reported on November 26, 2026, that the E-LAUTE project has put a digital edition online through the Austrian National Library. The address is the library's edition site. Pages of German lute tablature are shown in full, and software turns them into other tablature formats and into modern staff notation.

In the 1500s the lute was the instrument for a small room: it accompanied singing and dancing and also carried solo pieces. Tablature wrote the music as positions on the fingerboard, which made pieces easier to learn and to copy. As other instruments took its place, the code faded. Printed books, manuscripts, and teaching manuals in that notation are now scattered through European libraries. Until recently only a few specialists could play them.

Kateryna Schöning, the Austrian project lead at the University of Music and Performing Arts Vienna, said the edition also keeps the words that travel with the music. Recordings by players from the Schola Cantorum Basiliensis in Basel sit next to the scores. Cordula Kropik, at the University of Bayreuth, who works on the texts, called the launch an interim step. More sources still need to be edited, and single manuscripts still need a fuller historical setting. The news is that a first shelf of this music can be read and heard. It is not the whole surviving repertoire.
