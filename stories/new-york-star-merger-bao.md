Astronomers reconstructed a stellar merger from before it began through the weeks just after, NASA Science reported on November 18, 2026. A star a little less massive than the Sun spiraled into a puffy supergiant at the edge of the Andromeda galaxy. The team nicknamed the single star that remained Bao. Viraj Karambelkar, a NASA Hubble Fellow at Columbia University in New York and first author of a paper published Wednesday in Nature, said mergers are hard to catch because they are hard to predict. The group saw something unusual in 2023 and kept watching. In 2025 the system erupted. The James Webb Space Telescope observed it 47 days after the peak, the earliest follow-up of this kind, and that view helped describe the pair before, during, and just after the merger.

Before the outburst the primary star was about seven times the Sun's mass, 350 times its radius, and 18,000 times as bright. Once the smaller star touched the giant, the merger took about seven orbits, roughly seven years, rather than the thousands of orbits seen when two smaller stars merge. Webb's mid-infrared data matched dust the giant had shed over centuries and showed the stars had become one. That is not the only way these encounters end. In other systems both stars survive in a tighter orbit. This one did not.
