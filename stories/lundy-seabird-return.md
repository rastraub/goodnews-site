Seabirds on Lundy, a small island in the Bristol Channel, are a rare upward line in a bad decade for British seabirds. Mongabay's video, published November 21, 2026, reports that rats which had reached the island were taking eggs and chicks, and that numbers were about 6,000 around the time a decision was made, 20 years ago, to remove them. A warden in the film puts the count now at about 48,000, naming puffins, razorbills, guillemots, Manx shearwaters, storm petrels, fulmars, and kittiwakes among the birds that nest there.

The water had a parallel story. The seas around Lundy became the United Kingdom's first marine nature reserve, and in 2003 a rectangle of the east coast became the country's first no-take zone, where fishing is off limits. The film says islanders, North Devon people, local fishers, and conservationists worked out together what would still be allowed in the wider protected area, including rod fishing and lobster pots, while trawling and dredging stayed out. A fisherman who spent decades potting for lobster and crab said a string of pots outside the zone might yield a dozen lobsters, while inside it a night could bring many more, and larger ones. Studies cited in the video found lobsters in the zone both bigger and more numerous, with some spilling into fished water. The marine protected area was aimed in particular at spiny lobsters, also called crawfish, which the film says were brought back from the brink. The counts are what the island's people told Mongabay. They are not a national seabird census.
