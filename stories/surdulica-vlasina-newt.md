What field guides called the alpine newt is five species, Phys.org reported on November 12, 2026, from genetic work led by Ben Wielstra of Naturalis and Leiden University. The animals look almost the same. Their DNA does not. Three of the five now occur in the Netherlands, including newts from the Balkans and Italy that people introduced there.

One of the five had never been described. It lives on the Vlasina Plateau, on the border between Serbia and Bulgaria. The journal Zootaxa gave it the name Mesotriton similis. The authors' English name is the Vlasina alpine newt. Similis means similar. Wielstra said that even though the newts look alike to us, crosses between the species produce young that are much less likely to survive. He and Serbian colleagues called this the rarest alpine newt in Europe, with a limited range and a small population, and wrote that it is highly vulnerable to pollution, habitat damage, and introduced fish.

The genus name is not settled. Some amphibian specialists argue the group should be Ichthyosaura rather than Mesotriton, and the authors left open a future rename. The split into five species does not wait on that argument. The news is the name and the map, not a new count of newts in the water.
