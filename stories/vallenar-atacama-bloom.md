Noticias Ambientales reported on November 3, 2026, that the Atacama Desert in northern Chile is in one of its widest flowerings in decades after winter rains tied to El Niño. Chile's national forestry corporation, CONAF, estimates the bloom covers about 17,000 square kilometres, about 23 percent of the Atacama region. More than 200 native species are up, including añañuca, lion's claw, guanaco's paw, and the jote flower, in fuchsia, violet, yellow, white, and pink.

In a year without El Niño, much of this coast gets about 2 millimetres of rain. Between July and September, some towns recorded far more. Vallenar received as much as 183.5 millimetres. Seeds, bulbs, and rhizomes sit dormant under the sand until that moisture arrives, then finish a life cycle in a few weeks. The same storms also caused floods and landslides that damaged homes in parts of the north. The flower report treats that damage as part of the same weather, not as the story of the bloom.

Most of the flowers are expected to disappear during November as the desert dries. A few drought-tolerant species may hold on until January. Authorities expect a busy season for visitors. The news is the extent CONAF has measured and the plants already in flower. It is not a promise that the same carpet will return next year.
