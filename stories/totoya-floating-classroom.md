Children on the remote Fijian island of Totoya spent three days learning their own place from the people who know it. RNZ reported on November 26, 2026, that 45 students from the island's four villages joined a pilot run by Conservation International. They wove food baskets, built a model of the island, and sailed on Uto ni Yalo, a double-hulled voyaging canoe.

Regional project manager Catherina Bolinga said one elder on board was the last surviving sailor from Totoya. He named the parts of the canoe for the children and taught them to weave baskets, which they filled with food and carried aboard. Elders told her it was the first time they had taught that weaving to the younger generation, because they usually did it themselves.

Totoya District School head teacher Jone Draunidalo said the pilot changed how teachers plan to work. He gave measuring the playground, for area and perimeter, as the kind of lesson that can leave the classroom, and said the school would start using what it learned next year. Conservation International Fiji director Semisi Meo said knowledge survives only when it is practiced, and that island communities are losing both nature and skills as people move away.

The approach follows Guardians, a Samoa project from 2018 that used a voyaging canoe as a classroom. Those Samoan lessons are now supplementary material in the primary curriculum. The same idea has been piloted on Moala in Fiji and on Mauke, Mangaia, and Atiu in the Cook Islands, with support from New Zealand's Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Trade and the Global Environment Facility. Those funded projects end in the middle of next year. Matuku, the last of three Fijian pilot islands, is due to host the programme early next year, and Fiji's education ministry is expected to help turn the lessons into modules. The news is the Totoya pilot. It is not yet a new national curriculum.
