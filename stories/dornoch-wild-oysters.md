The Guardian reported on November 15, 2026, that European flat oysters are producing young again in the Dornoch Firth, north of Inverness, at a site where they had been locally extinct for more than a century. The babies are about the size of a 50 pence piece. Professor Bill Sanderson of Heriot-Watt University, who leads the Dornoch Environmental Enhancement Project, called the breeding a significant milestone. It is the first time in the UK, the paper said, that scientists have bred wild oysters at a place the species had vanished from for that long.

The project, launched 12 years ago with Glenmorangie and the Marine Conservation Society, has deposited about 140,000 adult oysters. Volunteers hand-clean them first. The shellfish come from Loch Ryan, near Stranraer, a surviving bed of about 23.5 million oysters. Research associate Gurjameer Ramday found the juveniles and said warmer seas in the last two years had helped. Sanderson said a reef is very likely to double seabed diversity over a decade. The team hopes for a self-sustaining population by 2031, and it could be a decade or more before any fishing. The news is the babies, not a harvest.
