New Zealand's largest gathering of ocean-going canoes in generations is underway on Doubtless Bay, and the point is to pass the skills on. RNZ reported on November 27, 2026, that Te Hekenga Mai, the fourth Te Hau Kōmaru festival and the first held in Northland, has been running all week at Aurere, Taipā, and Mangōnui. Six waka from around the country are there, with two double-hulled wa'a from Hawaii and one from Vanuatu. Voyagers from Tahiti, Samoa, and Rarotonga are taking part. Coordinator Joyce Munns Conrad expected more than 2,000 people, including about 1,000 children from Far North schools.

The beach is where the late Sir Hekenukumai Busby lived, built the voyaging canoe Te Aurere in the early 1990s, and taught navigation at his Kupe Waka Centre. The modern revival is often traced to 1985, when the Hawaiian canoe Hōkūleʻa reached Waitangi and Sir James Henare challenged Māori to build their own. Busby sailed Te Aurere to Rarotonga in 1992. At this festival, children can learn the stars, the Māori lunar calendar, canoe rigs, knots, and wayfinding from the wind and the water.

Stan Conrad, now captain of Te Aurere and the only Māori aboard Hōkūleʻa when it landed in 1985, told RNZ that waka and language connect the islands of the Pacific. Bruce Blankenfeld, director of voyaging for the Polynesian Voyaging Society, was on that 1985 journey. The news is six days of teaching on the water. It is not a new national school subject.
