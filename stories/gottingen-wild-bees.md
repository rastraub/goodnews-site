Phys.org reported on November 18, 2026, that a University of Göttingen team found wild bees richest on calcareous grasslands that are managed, but not intensively. The study, published in the Journal of Applied Ecology, covered 96 sites in Estonia, Germany, and Spain over two years. Researchers recorded 6,716 wild bees in 235 species. Diversity peaked at low to moderate use, partly because those meadows held more kinds of plants. In two of the three regions, bees also did better when other chalk grasslands sat nearby.

Lead author Ira Hannappel said traditional use of these meadows often no longer pays, so many are being abandoned and then grow over with scrub. The team also looked at European agri-environment payments. In Germany, payments tied to farming helped the bees on these grasslands only where chalk grassland was already common in the surrounding landscape. The authors said there is no single recipe. Protecting and restoring the grasslands themselves comes first, and payments can add to that only in some places. The result is a count and a pattern. It is not a claim that the meadows have been saved.
