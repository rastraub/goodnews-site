Maribel Pacco is not a biologist by training, and she can still name ten Andean bears on her community's reserve. Mongabay reported on November 20, 2026, that the Quechua community of Pillco Grande has identified 10 individual spectacled bears in Pumataki, a private conservation area in the buffer of Manu National Park, in Peru's Cusco region. The bears are told apart by the pale facial marks that sit around the eyes like a personal pattern. Pacco leads the camera traps. She told Mongabay she decided to work in conservation after watching a mother and cubs feed on bromeliads at a forest edge.

The project is three years old. A 2023 grant from the Peruvian Society for Environmental Law, Conservamos por Naturaleza, helped her start the monitoring and classes in Quechua. Children see the footage, name the bears, and grow carrots, lettuce, onions, and cauliflower in a school garden. Mothers sell some of that produce. Community members go out in groups of five to keep the cameras working. The traps have also caught an oncilla, a dwarf brocket deer, a puma, opossums, and coatis. Rebecca Zug, a biologist in Quito who is not part of the project, told Mongabay that community-led work teaches people their own wildlife in a way an outside voice does not. Funding has forced Pacco to scale back some next steps. The cameras, the classes, and a new management committee are still going. Russ Van Horn, of the San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance, told Mongabay that Peru is thought to hold about 5,000 Andean bears. That is a range-wide estimate, not a count from these cameras.
