A snake from Ethiopia's Harenna Forest that had been folded into another species now has its own name, Phys.org reported on November 19, 2026. Arthur Tiutenko of Friedrich-Alexander University Erlangen-Nürnberg, with colleagues at LMU Munich and the South African National Museum, described Dasypeltis albigularis, the white-throated egg-eater, in the journal Herpetozoa. A local employee brought a young female to the team. At first she looked like Dasypeltis atra, the montane egg-eater, which is widespread in the eastern Congo Basin and had not been recorded in this forest.

The Harenna animal is black with a white throat that continues over about the first 60 belly scales, and blue skin showing between the scales. Other egg-eaters rub their scales to make a warning buzz. This female made the motion and stayed quiet. Egg-eaters live on eggs alone. Enlarged vertebrae reach into the throat and slice a shell open; the snake swallows the contents and spits the shell back out. The paper's account of hatchlings puts them at about 1.5 grams and 20 centimetres long, thin enough to need the eggs of small birds.

When the female was paired with a male montane egg-eater, one female hatched from 21 eggs, a poor result for a match inside one species. Genetics then separated the Harenna snake from D. atra. Tiutenko said three other egg-eaters from the Horn of Africa are still being worked up. Those are not named species yet. The published news is this one.
