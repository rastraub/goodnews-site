The Conversation reported on November 17, 2026, that invertebrates are recovering on Macquarie Island and Antipodes Island after invasive rodents were removed. Justine Shaw, of Queensland University of Technology and Adelaide University, wrote that Macquarie was declared free of rats, mice, and rabbits in 2014, and that Antipodes has been free of mice since 2018. Seabirds on cleared islands are already well counted. The new work asked what happened to the smaller animals.

Traps resampled the same sites and were compared with older records: about 30 years of data for Macquarie and eight for Antipodes. Overall, there were more invertebrates and, at most sites, more species. On Antipodes, where mice had been the only invasive mammal, spiders, beetles, worms, and pseudoscorpions showed the strongest gains. On Macquarie, native flies benefited most. Shaw wrote that rabbits had grazed Macquarie's grasslands and megaherbs for more than 150 years, and that the plants are still not fully recovered, which leaves less habitat. Antipodes has more than 80 known native insect species and 16 spiders. Macquarie has 19 known native insects and 3 spiders. The islands share 18 invertebrate species despite nearly 1,500 kilometres of ocean between them. A cave weta on Antipodes is likely extinct because of the mice. The paper's point is that removal helps, and that a complicated history of rabbits and rats makes the rebound slower. It is not a claim that every species has returned.
