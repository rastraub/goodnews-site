A doctoral student has done something small-cat specialists say almost nobody manages: she has caught, measured, and GPS-collared seven clouded oncillas in Costa Rica's Talamanca Mountains. Mongabay reported the work on November 24, 2026. Amaia Autor, a Ph.D. student at the University of Montana, started setting traps in the cold cloud forest in 2022. The first success was March 21, 2023. The cat was melanistic, black, with grey paws. She named it Calcetinas, Spanish for socks.

Wildlife veterinarian Natalie Montera, of Cuatro Gatos Conservación, confirmed to Mongabay that Autor is the first person to capture free-ranging animals of this Central American montane cat for research and to track them. Montera anesthetized several of the cats so Autor could fit a collar, take samples, and measure them before release. Tadeu de Oliveira, who leads the Tiger Cats Conservation Initiative, told Mongabay that catching any tiger cat is rare. He said even a few months of locations would be information nobody else has, and that seven animals is a strong sample.

The cats are about the size of a house cat, usually 2 kilograms or less, and live above about 1,500 metres in Costa Rica and Panama. Camera traps often confuse them with ocelots or margays. Autor told Mongabay that early tracks show they stay in forest and rarely cross open pasture or towns, which is why connected forest matters. The collars run on double-A batteries and collect GPS data for up to four months, so the mapped range is still short. She said she has another year or two of analysis before the thesis, and the work is not published. Adviser Mark Hebblewhite said landowners, ranchers, and teachers around the mountains are now asking for places where animals can cross the Pan-American Highway. The news is seven collars and a community that knows the cat is there. It is not yet a published map of how far the cats roam.
