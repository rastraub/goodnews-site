A new paper describes Bikar and Bokak, two remote atolls in the Marshall Islands, as some of the most intact ecosystems left in the region. Phys.org reported the study on November 11, 2026. It was published in Frontiers in Marine Science. Lead author Alan Friedlander, a marine ecologist at the University of Hawaiʻi who was chief scientist for a Pristine Seas expedition, said the work is the first full baseline of the national marine sanctuaries there, from the sky to the deep sea. He credited the community of Utirik Atoll with safeguarding the places for decades.

The atolls are the country's northernmost, about 116 and 385 kilometres from the nearest inhabited atoll. In 2023 the team surveyed seabirds, plants, turtle nesting, reefs, and deep water with scuba, video, cameras, and a submarine. They confirmed or judged likely 15 seabird colonies. They recorded at least 266 groups of reef fish, 138 groups of deeper fish, and 79 groups of deep-sea fishes, plus 15 groups of open-water predators such as sharks and jacks. Fourteen of the groups had not been recorded in the Marshall Islands before, and two might be species new to science. The paper does not claim those two are confirmed new species. Turtle nesting was almost entirely at Bikar, where the team counted 132 green turtle nest pits. Bokak had higher reef-fish biomass and more coral-dominated reefs. About 48,000 square kilometres around the atolls were declared the country's first national marine sanctuary two years after the survey. The news is the published baseline. It is one visit, not a promise that the reefs will stay this healthy.
