What scientists had been calling the Alpine newt is five species. Phys.org reported the finding on November 12, 2026, from genetic work by Leiden biologists. The animals look almost the same, including to a trained eye, but their DNA separates them. One of the five had not been described before. It lives on the Vlasina Plateau, on the border between Serbia and Bulgaria, and it received the name Mesotriton similis when the description was published in Zootaxa. The authors suggested "Vlasina Alpine newt" as the English name. Biologist Ben Wielstra, of Naturalis and Leiden University, said similis means similar, and that genetically this newt is at least as different from the original Dutch newts as a person is from a chimpanzee. Mating across these species produces young that are much less likely to survive.

Three of the five now occur in the Netherlands, because animals from the Balkans and Italy have also been introduced there. Wielstra and his Serbian colleagues wrote that the new species is the rarest Alpine newt in Europe. Its small range and small population leave it open to pollution, habitat damage, and fish that people put into its water. The authors also left the genus name open. Amphibian experts still disagree on whether this group should be called Mesotriton or Ichthyosaura. The result is a split of animals that already exist. It is not a claim that any of the five has suddenly become common.
