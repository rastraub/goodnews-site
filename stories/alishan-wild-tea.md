Mongabay reported on November 12, 2026, that a few oolong growers in Taiwan's Alishan hills have given up chemical farming as the weather has turned against the crop. Local tea production fell about 13.5 percent between 2014 and 2023. Alishan warmed about 0.7 degrees Celsius in a decade, nearly three times Taiwan's average, and the mist that used to shade the leaves has been thinning. Huang Cho-ying, of National Taiwan University, said growers have faced two or three spring droughts in five years, just when the plants need water.

Lin Kuang-li, 60, a sixth-generation grower in Longyanlin, dropped pesticides about 30 years ago. He grows alfalfa as green manure and mulches with cut branches. He said his soil holds and drains water better than ground treated with chemical fertilizer, and that a bush has to learn the climate or it gets sick. In Taihe, Chang Chih-tsung, 51, and Yeh Hsin-wen, 45, leave Jhin-hsüan oolong unpruned, unwatered, and unsprayed under the forest. Yeh said the plants root deeper and survived droughts that killed irrigated bushes. They harvest 24 to 36 kilograms a year and rent a guesthouse to make up the income. Geographer Jiang Hong said fewer than 10 Alishan growers have made the switch, because the yield is lower. The news is those farms. It is not a change across the whole tea region.
