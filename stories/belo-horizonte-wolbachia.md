Children in parts of Belo Horizonte where Wolbachia mosquitoes took hold had fewer dengue and chikungunya infections than children in comparison neighborhoods. Emory University reported the EVITA trial on its news site after the team presented the results on November 21, 2026, at the American Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene meeting. The results will be submitted for peer review. They are not yet a journal paper.

The trial followed 5,757 schoolchildren, ages 6 to 11, from 2020 through 2024 in 58 school-area clusters. Twenty-nine clusters received releases of Aedes aegypti mosquitoes carrying the wMel strain of Wolbachia, plus the city's usual sprays and efforts to remove breeding sites. Twenty-nine received only the usual measures. Releases ran from January 2021 through May 2022. Children gave a blood sample in 2020 and then once a year through 2024. The labs looked for antibodies to dengue, Zika, and chikungunya, which show that an infection happened. They do not, by themselves, say how sick a child became.

Sri Edupuganti, a co-principal investigator at Emory, said overall efficacy against arboviral infection was 68.6 percent. For dengue it was 68.5 percent, and for chikungunya 73.3 percent. Luciano Moreira, of Fiocruz and the World Mosquito Program in Brazil, said a locally adapted strain stayed established for more than three years. The trial covered 2024, which Emory described as Brazil's largest recorded dengue outbreak. The news is this difference in infections in one city. It is not a claim that dengue has been eliminated, and it is not yet a reviewed paper.
