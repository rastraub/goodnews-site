Whales are feeding again in waters at the southern end of Argentina where sightings had thinned for decades. Mongabay reported on November 27, 2026, that a local project in the Beagle Channel has identified 208 humpbacks since 2013 and has recorded a steady rise in sightings since at least 2018. A female called Shima has returned for eight years running. A young male called Buddha was seen again after several years away. A 2025 collaborative count, built from photographs taken by boat crews, guides, photographers, and tourists, identified 22 humpbacks in the channel, 17 of them new to the catalog. The animals are there to eat squat lobster and Falkland sprat. The markings on the tail are how researchers tell one whale from another.

Researchers said the return may reflect a wider recovery after bans on commercial whaling. They also said more whales in a busier channel means more risk of being hit by ships, including cruise traffic bound for Antarctica. Mongabay noted climate change, krill fishing, and offshore oil and gas plans as further pressure. Those are limits on the good news, not a reason to skip the counts.

Farther up the Patagonian coast, sei whales, an endangered species and one of the largest animals on earth, are using the San Jorge Gulf, between Chubut and Santa Cruz, as a feeding ground after nearly a century of absence driven by hunting. Over 15 years the population has rebounded. Researchers have satellite tags on two whales. Early tracks show them lingering in the gulf from October to June, eating squat lobsters, copepods, and small fish, then heading north toward southern Brazil, where they usually breed. Two tags are a start of a map. They are not a census of every sei whale in the South Atlantic.
