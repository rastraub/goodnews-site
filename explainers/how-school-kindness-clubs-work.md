## What it is

A school kindness club is a small group of students with one adult sponsor. The work is specific and finishable: notes for cafeteria staff, a coat drive the counselor designed, a welcome for a new student, a fundraiser the office can receipt. A poster that says "be kind" is a decoration. The club is the list of what the students will do on a real day, and who will clean up afterward.

The [Random Acts of Kindness Foundation](https://www.randomactsofkindness.org/for-educators) publishes free classroom materials, and its educator page includes a guide to starting a kindness club, along with lesson plans. Those resources are a starting kit, not a franchise you have to join. A club can be three eighth graders and a teacher. It does not need matching T-shirts to be real.

Students already do this work without the word "club": a team that brings shoes, a student who shares a prize so classmates can eat, a long project that puts books or toys into the right hands. The club is a way to make the next project easier to approve, because the school has seen the last one done well.

## How it works

One adult says yes to being the sponsor. That person is the bridge to the principal. The students write a short plan: who is helped, what the students will do, when, what it costs, and how they will avoid putting another child on the spot. The principal, or whoever approves student activities, signs off. Then the club does the one project. Then it picks the next one.

Good projects have a recipient who agreed. A nursing home, a pantry, or a janitorial staff can say what would actually help. A classmate who uses a wheelchair does not need a surprise party planned about them in the hallway. Ask, privately, or help in a way that does not center their body as the lesson.

Meetings can be short. Twenty minutes at lunch is enough to assign jobs: who makes the sign, who talks to the office about money, who is the person shy students can text if speaking up is hard. Include those quiet jobs on purpose. A club that only rewards the loudest volunteer teaches the wrong lesson.

The foundation's [main site](https://www.randomactsofkindness.org/) is full of prompts. Use them as a menu, then cut the menu down to what your school can host. A calendar full of themes and no finished project is how these clubs die.

## How to start or help

Students: ask a teacher you trust, and bring one concrete idea, not ten. Teachers: you can hand the planning to the students and still keep the boundaries. Parents: the help that works is supplies the sponsor requested, or a ride, not a takeover of the meeting.

Skip anything that needs a child's medical story, a family's finances, or a photo of someone crying. Those go viral and they are not kind. The durable version is boring in the best way: permission, a budget that touches the school account, a date, and a thank-you to the people who did the work, including the ones who stayed late to stack boxes.

If the club raises money, decide the recipient before the posters go up, and say it in one sentence a student can repeat. Changing the cause after people have given is how trust breaks. Finish the thing you announced.
