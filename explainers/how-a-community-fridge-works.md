## What it is

A community fridge is a refrigerator in a public place that anyone can open. It might stand outside a shop, inside a library, or beside a church. Neighbors leave food that is still good to eat. Other neighbors take what they need. Most fridges do not ask for a form, a signup, or a library card. The idea is small on purpose: move food to someone who will eat it before it is thrown away.

[Freedge](https://freedge.org/), a network that maps these fridges, describes them as public refrigerators used to share food in a neighborhood. A host, often a business, a library, or a volunteer group, provides the appliance, the electricity, and a person who checks it. Many sites add a shelf for dry goods or a freezer beside the fridge. The rules are usually taped to the door.

This is not a food bank. A food bank is an organization that collects food in bulk and sends it out through pantries and meal programs. A fridge is one box, restocked by whoever walks past. Towns often have both. If you want the warehouse-and-pantry version, read [How food banks rescue food](/explainers/how-food-banks-rescue-food).

## How it works

Someone has to own the spot. The property owner agrees to the fridge, the power, and the foot traffic. A caretaker looks inside on a schedule, wipes spills, and throws out food that the rules do not allow or that no longer looks safe. Donors might be gardeners, neighbors cleaning out a cupboard, or a grocer with surplus. People who take food do not have to explain themselves. That privacy is part of the point.

The host decides what the fridge accepts. Sealed groceries and whole produce are the usual yes. Raw meat, home-canned food, and anything that has been sitting in a hot car are a usual no, unless that fridge's own rules say otherwise and the food was kept cold the whole time. Read the sign. If there is no sign, ask the host before you leave a meal.

## How to help

Use a fridge that is already running before you start a new one. Leave food the rules allow, or take what you need. Taking food is not stealing. It is the other half of the project.

If you want to start one, find a host with a safe spot, a working outlet, and a caretaker who will actually show up. Ask the local health department what a public fridge is allowed to hold where you live. Those rules are local, and they are not the same in every city. Post the rules in plain language, including what to do when the power goes out. [Freedge keeps a map](https://freedge.org/locations/) of fridges people have listed. A fridge that is not on the map can still be real. The map is a way to find one, not a license.

Cold food has to stay cold. The U.S. Department of Agriculture's Food Safety and Inspection Service says bacteria grow fastest between 40°F and 140°F, and that a refrigerator should be set at 40°F or below. Their [refrigeration guide](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/refrigeration) is the reference for that temperature rule. A community fridge follows the same science, plus whatever the local health department adds.
