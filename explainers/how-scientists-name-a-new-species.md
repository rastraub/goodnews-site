## What it is

Every known living thing has a two-part scientific name, like *Balaenoptera musculus* for the blue whale. The [Natural History Museum in London](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/what-is-a-type-specimen.html) explains that the first part is the genus, the group of close relatives, and the second part identifies the species itself. Using the same name everywhere means a scientist in Brazil and one in Japan know they are talking about the same animal.

The rules come from international codes: the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature for animals, and a separate code for plants, algae, and fungi. Common names like "blackbird" are not covered.

## How it works

**1. Compare.** A researcher who suspects a new species studies its body, and often its DNA, sound, or behavior, against its closest known relatives to show how it is different.

**2. Describe.** The main step, as one museum curator told the [Natural History Museum](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/what-is-a-type-specimen.html), is a written description of what the species looks like and how it differs from relatives.

**3. Pick a holotype.** The description must name at least one specimen to represent the species. Under the [zoological code](https://code.iczn.org/types-in-the-species-group/article-73-name-bearing-types-fixed-in-the-original-publication-holotypes-and-syntypes/), the holotype is the single specimen the new name is based on, and only the original author can choose it, in the original publication. The code recommends recording details like where and when it was collected, who collected it, and which collection holds it. Holotypes usually go to museums and herbaria, where they are kept, ideally forever, so later scientists can compare new finds against them. Other specimens studied alongside it, called paratypes, can become important if a holotype is ever lost, as the [American Museum of Natural History](https://www.amnh.org/explore/news-blogs/type-specimens-guide) notes happened to some bird types in World War II.

**4. Publish.** The description has to appear in a publicly available journal or data source so other scientists can check it.

**5. First one wins.** Under the [principle of priority](https://code.iczn.org/validity-of-names-and-nomenclatural-acts/article-23-principle-of-priority/), the oldest valid name for a species is generally the official one. A later name for the same species becomes a synonym.

The fun part is the name itself. Names can honor a place, a feature, or a person, and many new species are named for the local communities, students, or volunteers who helped find them.

## How to help

You do not need a lab to help. Photos shared on community science platforms like [iNaturalist](https://www.inaturalist.org/) help researchers track where species live, and sometimes lead them to ones nobody has named yet.
