## What it is

Hands-Only CPR is CPR without mouth-to-mouth breaths. The [American Heart Association](https://international.heart.org/en/resuscitation/hands-only-cpr) recommends it for anyone who sees a teen or adult suddenly collapse, and boils it down to two steps: call 911, then push hard and fast in the center of the chest.

It matters because cardiac arrest is common and usually happens away from a hospital. The AHA's [CPR facts and stats](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resources/cpr-facts-and-stats) say more than 350,000 cardiac arrests happen outside a hospital in the U.S. each year, and most of them, 73.4%, happen at home. Immediate CPR can double or triple a person's chance of survival, yet only about 40% of people get bystander CPR before professionals arrive.

## How it works

When a teen or adult suddenly collapses from cardiac arrest, the heart is usually quivering in an abnormal rhythm instead of pumping. The [AHA explains](https://international.heart.org/en/resuscitation/hands-only-cpr) that for the first few minutes there is still enough oxygen in the person's lungs and blood. What they need most is someone to push on the chest and keep that blood moving to the brain and heart, with as few interruptions as possible.

That is why the AHA's [2025 fact sheet](https://www.heart.org/en/-/media/Files/Nation-of-Lifesavers/2025-CPR-Fact-Sheet.pdf) says that for adults, Hands-Only CPR has been shown to be as effective in the first few minutes as CPR with breaths for cardiac arrest at home, at work, or in public. The beat to aim for is 100 to 120 pushes a minute, the tempo of "Stayin' Alive."

An AED, a device that can shock a heart back into a normal rhythm, adds to the chance of survival. If one is nearby, turn it on and follow its voice prompts.

## How to help

You do not need a certificate to help. In a [February 2026 AHA survey](https://newsroom.heart.org/news/new-consumer-survey-shows-many-still-incorrectly-believe-cpr-requires-special-training), nearly six in ten American adults wrongly believed only people with special training should do Hands-Only CPR. The AHA says anyone can call 911 and push.

The best way to be ready is to watch the AHA's short [Hands-Only CPR](https://cpr.heart.org/en/cpr-courses-and-kits/hands-only-cpr) videos, then take a class to learn breaths, AED use, and how to help children and infants. The AHA also encourages asking your school, workplace, faith community, or gym to make a cardiac emergency plan and get an AED.
