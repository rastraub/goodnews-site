## What it is

In the United States, a new medicine cannot be sold until the Food and Drug Administration, or FDA, decides it is safe and effective for its intended use. The [FDA describes the path](https://www.fda.gov/patients/learn-about-drug-and-device-approvals/drug-development-process) in five steps: discovery and development in the lab, preclinical research, clinical research in people, FDA review, and safety monitoring after the medicine reaches patients.

So when a story in this archive says "the FDA approved" a treatment, it usually means years of work by researchers, volunteers, and reviewers have just paid off.

## How it works

Before any person takes a new medicine, the company or research group, called the sponsor, must send the FDA an [Investigational New Drug application](https://www.fda.gov/patients/drug-development-process/step-3-clinical-research). It includes animal and lab safety data, how the medicine is made, and the study plans. The FDA has 30 days to review it and can pause the study, called a clinical hold, if volunteers would face unreasonable risk. The FDA says holds are rare; more often it suggests ways to improve the study.

Then come the [clinical trial phases](https://www.fda.gov/patients/drug-development-process/step-3-clinical-research):

- **Phase 1** usually involves 20 to 100 healthy volunteers or people with the condition, for several months, to study safety and dosage.
- **Phase 2** involves up to several hundred people with the condition, for several months to two years, to look at whether it works and what side effects it has. The FDA estimates about 33% of drugs move on from here.
- **Phase 3** involves 300 to 3,000 people for one to four years. These larger, longer studies provide most of the safety data and are more likely to catch rare side effects. About 25 to 30% of drugs move on.

If the evidence holds up, the sponsor files a [New Drug Application](https://www.fda.gov/patients/drug-development-process/step-4-fda-drug-review) that tells the medicine's full story, from lab data to Phase 3 results and proposed labeling. Once the FDA accepts it as complete, the review team has 6 to 10 months to decide. Doctors, statisticians, chemists, and other specialists each review their part, inspectors visit study sites to check the data, and sometimes an outside advisory committee, including a patient representative, weighs in. A senior FDA official makes the final call.

Approval is not the end. The FDA keeps watching for problems after a medicine is in use, and some companies run Phase 4 studies with thousands more patients.

## How to help

Medicines only reach approval because volunteers join studies. If you or someone you care for is curious, you can search [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/) for studies that are recruiting, then talk it over with your doctor.
