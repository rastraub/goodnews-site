## What it is

A therapy animal visits people with a handler. The visit might be a hospital room, a library reading hour, a nursing home, or a classroom. The animal is a guest. It does not live with the people it visits, and it is not trained to perform a disability-related task for one handler the way a service dog is. The job is the visit itself: a calm animal, a short stay, and a person who can leave when the visit should end.

[Pet Partners](https://petpartners.org/) is a national group that educates, evaluates, and registers handler-and-animal teams and provides insurance for visits. Facilities often require that kind of registration, or their own screening, before a team can walk in. A friendly dog and a wish to help are not the same credential.

The U.S. Department of Justice explains service animals on its [ADA page](https://www.ada.gov/topics/service-animals/). A service animal is trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability. Therapy animals, emotional support animals, and pets are not that category. The difference matters in a doorway. A service dog goes where its handler has a right to go, and strangers leave it alone. A therapy team goes only where it was invited.

## How it works

A handler and an animal practice basic manners: the animal can be around wheelchairs, noises, and strangers without lunging or relieving itself indoors. A registering group, or the hospital's own program, evaluates the pair. If they pass, a facility schedules them. Visits are short. Patients and students can opt out. The handler watches the animal for stress and ends the visit early if either of them is done.

Horses and ponies sometimes do this work too, usually at a barn or a carefully planned outdoor visit, not in a hallway. Those programs have their own insurance and their own rules about who may lead an animal. A pony in a parking lot because someone thought it would be sweet is not a program.

Schools that want a reading dog ask the district which teams are allowed. The teacher plans for allergies, fear, and a student who wants to sit out. Families hear about the visit beforehand. The dog's handler, not the class, decides when the dog needs a break.

## How to help

If you and your animal want to volunteer, start with a registering group such as Pet Partners or with the volunteer office of the hospital you hope to visit. Expect a class, an evaluation, and a wait. Do not show up at a nurses' station with a pet carrier.

If you want a visit for a class, a library, or a ward, ask the facility which programs it already approves. They may have a list. Adding your cousin's dog to that list is their decision, made before the day, not at the door.

You can also help without an animal. Some programs need people to schedule visits or to sit with a patient who wants the dog and also wants a person nearby. And if what you actually need is a service dog for one handler, that is a different, longer training path. A therapy visit will not become that dog at the end of the hour.

When a therapy animal is in the room, the courtesy is simple. Ask. Sit. Let the person who is scared skip it. Thank the handler. Then let them leave on time.
