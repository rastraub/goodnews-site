## What it is

Sea turtle rescue is the work of finding sick, injured, entangled, or stranded sea turtles, getting them to care, and returning the healthy ones to the ocean. In the United States, much of it runs through the [Sea Turtle Stranding and Salvage Network](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/national/marine-life-distress/sea-turtle-stranding-and-salvage-network). NOAA Fisheries formally set up the network in 1980 to document strandings from Maine to Texas and parts of the U.S. Caribbean. NOAA coordinates it, but the people who answer the calls are local partners: state agencies, aquariums, and permitted nonprofits.

Every sea turtle in U.S. waters is listed as threatened or endangered. That is why rescue is a permitted job, not a free-for-all. The network's partners respond to turtles, collect scientific data, move sick and injured animals to licensed rehabilitation centers, and teach the public what to do.

## How it works

A rescue usually starts with a phone call. Someone on a beach or a boat sees a turtle in trouble and calls the local hotline listed on [NOAA's reporting page](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/report). A trained responder comes out, checks the turtle, and decides what happens next.

Late fall is the busiest season in New England. Sea turtles are reptiles, so their body temperature follows the water. [Mass Audubon](https://www.massaudubon.org/places-to-explore/wildlife-sanctuaries/wellfleet-bay/projects/sea-turtles) explains that some young turtles get trapped by the hooked shape of Cape Cod as the water cools. Below about 55°F they become "cold-stunned," too cold to swim or eat, and onshore winds push them onto the beaches of Cape Cod Bay. Most strandings there happen from November to mid-January, and most of the turtles are juvenile Kemp's ridleys, the smallest and rarest sea turtle.

Mass Audubon's Wellfleet Bay staff and more than 200 trained volunteers patrol those beaches day and night. A rescued turtle goes to the sanctuary first so its body temperature can stabilize. Then volunteers drive it to a rehabilitation center, such as the New England Aquarium or the National Marine Life Center, where it is treated for injuries and illness. Most are eventually released back into the wild, often in warmer water.

The data matters too. Each state's network sends records to a central database, which helps scientists see what is hurting turtles and where.

## How to help

The most useful thing most people can do is know the number before they need it. Look up your coast's hotline on [NOAA's reporting page](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/report) and save it in your phone. If you find a turtle, keep people and pets back, call, and do what the responder tells you.

On Cape Cod, [Mass Audubon](https://www.massaudubon.org/places-to-explore/wildlife-sanctuaries/wellfleet-bay/projects/sea-turtles) gives specific steps for cold-stunned turtles: do not assume it is dead, do not put it back in the water, move it above the high-tide line without holding the head or flippers, cover it with seaweed (never sand), mark the spot, and call the Wellfleet Bay hotline. Those steps are for that program. Elsewhere, ask your local hotline first.

If you want to do more, ask the network partner near you whether it trains beach-patrol or transport volunteers, and give through that organization's own website.
