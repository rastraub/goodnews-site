## What it is

Search and rescue, or SAR, is two jobs that often happen together. A rescue usually means responders know where someone is and need to get them out safely, like a hiker with a broken leg on a ledge. A search means nobody knows where the person is. [Rocky Mountain National Park](https://www.nps.gov/romo/search_and_rescue.htm) explains that searches are harder in many ways for exactly that reason.

Who leads depends on where it happens. In national parks, rangers and the U.S. Park Police lead. The [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/orgs/aviationprogram/visitor-protection.htm) says it responds to about 4,500 SAR incidents a year, using roughly 50,000 to 100,000 personnel hours, from lost hikers to technical rescues high in the mountains. A [Congressional Research Service overview](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12020) notes that on lands where a state shares jurisdiction, park efforts usually support the local sheriff's office, and qualified volunteer SAR teams can assist under formal agreements.

## How it works

A search starts with one question: where was the person last seen? Searchers surround the area where the person is likely to be and block routes toward cliffs and fast rivers, especially for a child. [Rocky Mountain National Park](https://www.nps.gov/romo/search_and_rescue.htm) says quick, accurate reporting is one of the most helpful things a family can do, because the search area grows dramatically with every hour that passes.

Often a small, fast team goes out first. Larger groups follow, spreading out and calling the person's name or blowing whistles. If that does not work, teams switch to a slow, careful grid search. Searchers also check places that do not seem logical, because lost people do not always think the way they would on a calm day.

Tools help. The [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/orgs/aviationprogram/visitor-protection.htm) lists computer-based SAR programs, satellite technology, snow vehicles, and watercraft. Helicopters search from the air, ferry searchers closer, and in hard terrain can lift a person out by hoist or short-haul.

Kids can help searchers too. The [Hug-a-Tree program](https://mra.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/hugatree.pdf), carried by the National Association for Search and Rescue, teaches children to stay put, carry a whistle and a trash bag to stay dry, wear bright colors, and never hide from searchers.

## How to help

Teach the Hug-a-Tree rules before your next family hike, and always leave a trip plan with someone at home. If you want to serve, contact your county sheriff's office or a team listed by the [Mountain Rescue Association](https://mra.org/) and ask about training.
