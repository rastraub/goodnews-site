## What it is

A food bank is usually a warehouse organization. It takes in food from grocery stores, farms, drives, and sometimes government programs, stores it, and sends it on to pantries, kitchens, and mobile distributions. A pantry is the neighborhood place where a person picks up groceries. People mix the two words up. The bank is the hub. The pantry is the door a neighbor walks through.

Food rescue is the step that keeps edible food out of the trash. A grocer may have fruit that is ripe today and unsellable tomorrow. A restaurant may have prepared meals it cannot serve. A farm may have a crop that is ugly and still fine to eat. [Feeding America](https://www.feedingamerica.org/our-work/our-approach/reduce-food-waste), the national network of food banks, describes food rescue as one way that network keeps edible food from being wasted and moves it toward people who need it. Their [program page](https://www.feedingamerica.org/our-work/hunger-relief-programs) is the overview of how food and meals are offered. This page does not repeat their statistics. Those numbers change, and the current ones live on their site.

A community fridge is smaller and more informal. It is one public refrigerator, not a warehouse. Both can exist in the same town. The fridge version is explained in [How a community fridge works](/explainers/how-a-community-fridge-works).

## How it works

Food comes in from several directions. Stores and distributors donate surplus. Farms and gardens donate produce. Schools, offices, and scout troops hold drives. Some rescues pick up from restaurants on a route the health rules allow. At the warehouse, staff and volunteers check dates, sort out anything unsafe, and pack boxes or bags. Refrigerated trucks matter once meat, milk, or produce is involved. A pantry or a meal site then gives the food out, sometimes as groceries and sometimes as a hot meal.

[Feeding America](https://www.feedingamerica.org/take-action/volunteer) says local food banks in its network post volunteer shifts, and that the work includes sorting, packing, and distributing food. A shift is usually scheduled. Walking in with a car full of random cans can still be welcome, but only if someone is there to receive it and the food is the kind they can store.

Eligibility rules are local. One pantry uses an honor system. Another asks for an ID or an address. A volunteer should learn the rule and then follow it, without adding a speech at the door. The person picking up groceries does not owe the room a story.

## How to help

The useful gifts are specific. Ask the pantry which foods it is short of, then collect those. Money is often the most flexible gift, because a food bank can buy what donations skip, such as rice, produce, or diapers, at a price a household cannot match. Time matters on the sort line and at distributions. [Their volunteer page](https://www.feedingamerica.org/take-action/volunteer) is a way to find a food bank in the network. A pantry that is not in that network is still real. Its own website, or a posted phone number, is the way in.

Do not invent a parking-lot distribution of food you rescued from a dumpster or a closed restaurant. Temperature is the safety problem. The U.S. Department of Agriculture says bacteria grow fastest between 40°F and 140°F, and that refrigerators should stay at 40°F or below. That guidance is in the FSIS [refrigeration page](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/refrigeration). Food banks train people for this. A trunk in July is not a warehouse.

If a class wants a drive, make it a list the pantry wrote, with a date when someone from the pantry can receive the boxes. That is the whole project. It does not need a theme song.
